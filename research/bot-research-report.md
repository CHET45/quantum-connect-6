# Classical search, not a neural network, wins Quantum Connect

In every k-in-a-row competition we found that had a hard time limit, the winners were not neural networks trained from scratch but fast search: alpha-beta on bitboards with a transposition table, strong move ordering and a separate forced threat search. A cheap trained evaluation was added only on top of such a search. For Quantum Connect this conclusion is even stronger: **100 ms per call in plain Java rules out AlphaZero-sized networks**, and the board size is a parameter, so a network trained on one size is useless on another. But Quantum Connect is not Connect Four. According to the engine bytecode, it is a **gravity-based "six in a row" game on an 8×12 board** (Main runs 25×25) with a special move once every 10 of a player's own moves. The special move blows up a 3×3 block and compacts the columns, and **if after the explosion the board contains any line of six, the player who triggered the explosion wins, even if the line belongs to the opponent** ([engine bytecode](<notes/engine_rules_from_bytecode.md>)). Most of the research notes were written with Connect Four (4 in a row) in mind, so the knowledge transfers only selectively. Search engineering (bitboards, TT, iterative deepening, move ordering, time management) transfers almost entirely. Solved results, opening books, Pons's solver and Allis's parity theory do not transfer, or only partly. The closest analogues by win mechanics are Gomoku and Connect6 with their threat search (VCF/TSS). By destruction mechanics the closest are PopOut and atomic chess, but no source describes an AI for an explosion with a "the one who fired wins" rule. We should not train our own deep neural network. The only thing that pays off is a trainable pattern table over 6-cell windows (TD or Texel tuning), and even that only after a strong classical bot and a self-play harness are ready.

## The real rules: six in a row, a 3×3 explosion, and the player who triggers it wins

Before transferring other people's experience, we need to pin down what exactly we are playing. The rules below are read from the bytecode of `core-2.0-SNAPSHOT.jar`, and the coordinator re-checked the two key parameters (line length 6 and cooldown 10) ([engine bytecode](<notes/engine_rules_from_bytecode.md>)).

| Parameter | Classic Connect Four | Quantum Connect (engine) |
|---|---|---|
| Board | 7×6 | **8 rows × 12 columns** by default, set in the constructor; `Main` uses **25×25** |
| Win | 4 in a row | **6 in a row** in 4 directions, an "overline" (7+) also counts |
| Special moves | none | **3×3 explosion** centered on the landing cell, then gravity on all columns |
| Special move frequency | — | first on a player's **11th own move**, then every **10 own moves** |
| Who wins after an explosion | — | **the one who triggered it**, if the board has any 6-line of any color |
| Time | — | **100 ms** for `accelerateQuark` and another 100 ms for `useSpecialMove`, a fresh thread on every call |
| Timeout or exception | — | the move is **skipped** (not a loss) |
| Draw | board full | top row full or **1000 moves** in total |

The explosion mechanics matter more than they seem. The special move does not add a piece: it places one in the lowest free cell of column c and destroys a 3×3 square together with that piece itself. In column c itself only the top piece of the stack disappears, while in the neighboring columns c±1 up to three pieces at heights h−1, h, h+1 disappear. After that everything lying above falls down. The result: up to seven actual pieces of both colors are removed, and the neighboring columns **shift down by 1–3 cells**, which can line up a horizontal or a diagonal. Because `hasWinner()` does not check whose line it is, an explosion that "tops up" the opponent's almost finished line brings victory to the player who triggered it. The opposite rule appears in the PopOut description: there, a pop-out that creates a four for the opponent hands the win to the opponent ([Rarepike variants](https://rarepike.com/four/variants/)). In Quantum Connect it is the other way around, so **any nearly complete 6-lines of both colors are a weapon for whoever has the special move charged**.

Three conclusions follow from the rules, and they determine the whole assessment of transferability that comes next. The first conclusion: a line length of 6 with eight rows narrows the game sharply. A column holds only **3 vertical positions out of six**, a row holds 7 horizontal ones. On 8×12 this gives **134 winning windows** (56 horizontal, 36 vertical, 42 diagonal) against 69 fours on 7×6 (our count, from the board geometry). The theory of m,n,k-games without gravity says that "five in a row" is already a draw on 8×8 (Hsu et al., 2018), and "eight in a row" is a draw even on an infinite board ([Wikipedia: m,n,k-game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M,n,k-game)). There is no solved result for gravity-based "six". Our hypothesis: when both sides defend carefully with ordinary moves, winning is very hard, and **games between strong bots will be decided by special moves or end in a draw at the limit**. This is the first thing to check with self-play.

The second conclusion: the special move is frequent and predictable. If the board filled up without explosions, 96 cells would give 48 moves per player, that is **at least four special moves for each side** (moves 11, 21, 31, 41). Explosions remove pieces, so a game lasts longer and the 1000-move limit is reachable. The cooldown schedule is deterministic and known to both sides, so for search it is not uncertainty but a schedule that must be built into the position state.

The third conclusion is about engineering, but it is decisive against a weak field. A timeout costs a whole tempo. Requesting a special move after an invalid column crashes the engine with an `ArrayIndexOutOfBoundsException`. The board size must not be hardcoded. The engine does not swap sides. A bot that never loses a move and never crashes is already ahead of part of the field. The built-in `CheatingBot` loses every move precisely because of the timeout ([engine bytecode](<notes/engine_rules_from_bytecode.md>)).

## Winners of neighboring competitions: alpha-beta plus threat search everywhere, networks only as evaluation

The only public trace of Quantum Connect itself is a CERN Indico entry about "QuantumConnect: Particle Collision Challenge" on 5 May 2025 at Satakunta University of Applied Sciences (Pori, Finland). Participants wrote Java bots for a Connect 4-style tournament and had **45 minutes** to develop. The rules and winners were not found, and the page requires a CERN login, so the information comes from a search summary ([Indico contribution 6172921](https://indico.cern.ch/event/1441614/contributions/6172921)). A format with 45 minutes points to simple opponents. This is an assumption, but an important one: against heuristic bots, reliability and tactics decide, not fine positional evaluation.

| Competition | Conditions | Who won | With what | Transfer to QC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kaggle ConnectX | 6×7, 4 in a row, 2 s/move | solver + book (~1600, unverified); AlphaZero 1282–1368 (places 5–9) | Pons-style alpha-beta, a book of 19 plies | engineering yes; book and solution no |
| Gomocup 2023–2025 | 15×15/20×20, 5 in a row; fastgame 5 s/move | Rapfi | PVS + NNUE patterns + VCF at leaves | pattern-table idea and VCF yes; NNUE on AVX no |
| Gomocup 2012–2018 | same | Yixin | alpha-beta + deep threat search + handcrafted evaluation | yes, closest template |
| Connect6, ICGA Olympiad | 6 in a row, 2 stones per move | NCTU6, MoreThenFive and others | proof-based threat search (RZOP), alpha-beta/MTD(f) + VCF | partly: k=6 matches, 2 stones per move does not |
| CodinGame Connect 4 | 9×7, first-move "steal" rule | darkhorse64, RoboStac | MCTS + bitboards + solver; RoboStac has a small network | partly |
| CodinGame UTTT | 100 ms per move | Legend bots, including Java ones | MCTS + solver, 20–40 thousand simulations | Java engineering yes; MCTS debatable |
| Othello | — | Logistello (6:0 against the world champion) | alpha-beta + regression-fitted pattern tables | yes, pattern evaluation |
| Checkers | — | Chinook | alpha-beta + TT + history + endgame databases | search yes; databases no |

In Kaggle ConnectX the ladder of results rises almost monotonically with tactical accuracy. A greedy bot scored about 268, depth-5 minimax 873, pure MCTS (~20 thousand simulations per move) 1075, AlphaZero 1282, 9th place out of 225 teams ([arXiv 2210.08263](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.08263v1)). The PARL AlphaZero implementation claimed about 1368 points and 5th place ([PARL PR #282](https://github.com/PaddlePaddle/PARL/pull/282)). The best described agent, according to a search summary of the discussion, combined deep alpha-beta based on Pascal Pons's solver with a 19-ply book compressed to about 1 MB, and held around 1st-2nd place with a score of about 1600 ([Kaggle discussion 188305](https://www.kaggle.com/c/connectx/discussion/188305)). We could not read the page directly, so we treat the place and score as unverified. A naive DQN notebook scored 555.5, which is below the starting 600 ([phunghieu notebook](https://www.kaggle.com/code/phunghieu/connectx-with-deep-q-learning)). What transfers to Quantum Connect from this is the lesson "tactical accuracy matters more than a beautiful evaluation". The concrete recipe does not transfer: a book and a solution are possible only because 6×7 is solved, while 8×12 with six in a row and explosions is not solved and will not be.

Gomocup is closer to our game in the substance of winning, because it also has a long line on a large board. From 2012 to 2018 all the open leagues were won by **Yixin** (alpha-beta, deep threat search, handcrafted evaluation) ([Wikipedia: Gomocup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gomocup)). Since 2023 **Rapfi** has dominated: in 2024 and 2025 it won all the main leagues except Caro, including fastgame with 5 s per move ([Gomocup 2025](https://gomocup.org/results/gomocup-result-2025), [Gomocup 2024](https://gomocup.org/results/gomocup-result-2024)). The Rapfi paper describes PVS with pruning, a TT and VCF search at the leaves. The evaluation there is a Mixnet network, decomposed along four line directions and **distilled into a codebook of 397,488 line patterns** that is updated only for the stones that changed. On CPU this gives **about +400 Elo over Katagomo**, and the large network did not beat the medium one because it is more expensive to update ([arXiv 2503.13178](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13178v1)). Hence the main lesson: even in the neural network era the winner is alpha-beta, and the network is turned into a table of patterns along lines. For 6-in-a-row on 8×12 the direct analogue is a table of states of each 6-cell window. Rapfi's speed rests on AVX2 and int8 ([arXiv 2503.13178](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13178v1)), which is not available in Java (see the last section).

Connect6 matches us in line length (6) but not in the turn structure: it has two stones per move. The Olympiads were won by Taiwanese and Chinese programs: NCTU6 in 2006 and MoreThenFive in 2010, with alpha-beta/MTD(f) and VCF ([Chessprogramming: Connect6](https://chessprogramming.org/Connect6)). NCTU6's signature technology is the proof-based threat search RZOP, a development of Thomsen's lambda search. NCTU6 won 8 games out of 8 against strong Taiwanese players in 2009 ([NYCU: RZOP thesis](https://ir.lib.nycu.edu.tw/handle/11536/32262?locale=en)). Dependency-based search gave Connect6 a speedup of 4.12 times on average and up to 50 times on hard positions ([NYCU: DBS paper](https://ir.lib.nycu.edu.tw/handle/11536/125019)). What transfers to us is the idea of "search only threatening moves"; the machinery for two stones per move is not needed.

CodinGame shows that at ~100 ms per move things can go differently. In Connect 4 (9×7) strong players use MCTS on bitboards: darkhorse64 has about **2 million games per second** and a solver that "predicts the outcome 15–20 moves before the end". RoboStac uses an AlphaZero-style scheme with a small dense network ([CodinGame forum: Connect 4](https://forum.codingame.com/t/community-puzzle-connect-4/189435)). In Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe with a 100 ms limit, Legend was also reached by Java bots (jacek, luigi_gelato with 20 thousand+ simulations, dbf with 30–40 thousand) using MCTS with a solver, node pools and no allocations ([CodinGame forum: UTTT p.8](https://forum.codingame.com/t/ultimate-tic-tac-toe-puzzle-discussion/22616?page=8)). This is the strongest counterargument to the "alpha-beta only" thesis, and we examine it in the next section. Still, the common denominator of all the winners is one: **an ultra-fast game model plus a mechanism for proving outcomes** (a solver, VCF, MCTS-Solver), and not positional intuition alone.

Outside "k in a row" the picture is the same. Logistello beat the Othello world champion Murakami 6:0 in 1997 with alpha-beta and pattern tables fitted by regression ([Buro, Logistello](https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~mburo/log.html)). Chinook is built on iterative alpha-beta with a TT and the history heuristic, endgame databases and an opening book ([Wikipedia: Chinook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinook_(computer_program))). Endgame databases do not suit us: positions are non-monotonic and there are too many states. The approach of "trainable patterns inside alpha-beta" suits us directly.

## What transfers: bitboards, TT and threat search yes; Allis parity and solvers almost no

### Five techniques give most of the strength, and all of them are feasible in Java

The most detailed figures on the contribution of individual techniques are in Pascal Pons's tutorial on the Connect Four solver. "Center first" ordering reduced nodes and time by **more than 10 times** ([Pons, Part 5](https://blog.gamesolver.org/solving-connect-four/05-move-exploration-order/)). Bitboards gave **about 6× in speed** with the same nodes ([Pons, Part 6](https://blog.gamesolver.org/solving-connect-four/06-bitboard/)). The transposition table cut the Middle-Medium set from 644 ms and 40.4 million nodes to **52 ms and 1.73 million** ([Pons, Part 7](https://blog.gamesolver.org/solving-connect-four/07-transposition-table/)). Move ordering by the number of threats created reduced nodes by **up to 100×** on hard positions ([Pons, Part 10](https://blog.gamesolver.org/solving-connect-four/10-better-move-ordering/)). The filter "do not play under the opponent's winning cell; two playable threats of his means a loss" gave another multiple-fold reduction ([Pons, Part 9](https://blog.gamesolver.org/solving-connect-four/09-anticipate-losing-moves/)). The chess literature confirms this. According to Schaeffer, the combination of TT + history heuristic gives **about 99% of the achievable tree reduction** up to depth 8 ([Schaeffer 1989](https://search.worldcat.org/oclc/8557794501)). More than 90% of cutoffs happen on the first move if the ordering is good ([Chessprogramming: Move Ordering](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Move_Ordering)). The choice between PVS and MTD(f) changes the node count by less than 20% ([Plaat et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1404.1515.pdf)).

All these techniques are independent of line length, so they transfer to "six" directly. What has to be adapted is the form. Pons's bitboard (height+1 bits per column) takes 108 bits on 8×12 and does not fit into a single `long`: you need two `long`s or a row-by-row layout. On 25×25 you need an array of words. So it is more sensible to write a generic representation right away, with a list of windows built at startup from the actual `getNumberOfRows()/getNumberOfColumns()`. Pons's score scale ("22 minus the number of stones played") loses its meaning with explosions, because the game is no longer bounded by the number of cells. Instead you need a "mate in N plies from the root" score ([Pons, Part 2](https://blog.gamesolver.org/solving-connect-four/02-test-protocol/)). The TT key must include both cooldown counters: the same board with a different readiness of the special move is a different position. Chess handles this the same way, mixing castling rights into the Zobrist key ([Chessprogramming: Zobrist Hashing](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Zobrist_Hashing)).

Time management transfers from chess entirely: the soft limit is checked between iterations, the hard limit every N nodes, and on a cutoff the best move of the last completed iteration is returned ([Chessprogramming: Time Management](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Time_Management)). CodinGame's Java experience adds specifics. Switching from objects to arrays let one player raise the working limit from 60 to 90–95 ms with almost no timeouts ([CodinGame forum: Java tips](https://forum.codingame.com/t/java-codingame-tips/191870)). Advice from darkhorse64: "allocate nothing in the real-time loop" ([CodinGame forum: UTTT p.9](https://forum.codingame.com/t/ultimate-tic-tac-toe-puzzle-discussion/22616?page=9)). For our engine, the bytecode adds its own features: every call runs in a new thread with a cold JIT, and an abandoned thread keeps running. So we need our own deadline of about 60–70 ms, a `System.nanoTime()` check on a node counter and a guaranteed exit from the search.

### Allis's parity theory and Connect Four solvers are almost useless here

Classic Connect Four is solved: the first player wins with a move into the center ([Wikipedia: Connect Four](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connect_Four)). Allis's solution rests on nine zugzwang control rules (claimeven, baseinverse, vertical and so on) and on classifying threats by row parity: odd threats are valuable to the first player, even ones to the second ([MIT OCW ES.268](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/es-268-the-mathematics-in-toys-and-games-spring-2010/94a5d1da64c57cbc5fdf135d985445a9_MITES_268S10_Ses5_ConFour.pdf)). Tromp's table shows that all solved boards with even width and height and width ≥6 are a win for the second player, but it contains no 12-column boards ([Tromp](https://tromp.github.io/c4/c4.html)). For Quantum Connect these results have two limitations. The first: they are about 4 in a row. The second: all of Allis's rules assume that pieces are permanent and cells fill from the bottom in order. The special move breaks both conditions every 10 moves: it changes the number of pieces in three columns and thereby flips the parity of the cells each side will have to occupy. Threat parity remains a useful term of the evaluation, especially in the first 20 plies, when there are no special moves yet. But it can only be trusted on a horizon where neither side has an explosion charged, and its weight has to be set by tuning, not taken from theory.

Solvers and books transfer even worse. Tromp's database of 8-ply positions of 7×6 cost about 40,000 CPU-hours, and the solution of 9×6 took about 2·10¹³ positions and 2000 hours ([Tromp](https://tromp.github.io/c4/c4.html)). Even Pons's final solver spends about 5.5 s in C++ on hard starting positions of 7×6 ([Pons, Part 12](https://blog.gamesolver.org/solving-connect-four/12-lower-bound-transposition-table/)). On 96 cells with non-monotonic play and 100 ms per move, exact scoring is possible only for short forced sequences. An opening book on the Kaggle pattern is theoretically applicable to the first 20 plies without special moves, but its benefit is unclear. This is low priority.

### Gomoku threat search matters more than Connect Four's

Since the line length is 6, a "threat" in our game resembles a Gomoku one, not a Connect Four one. A "five" (5 of 6 in a window with a playable empty cell) is an immediate win on the next move. An "open four" with two possible completions is the analogue of an open four in Gomoku. Allis solved Gomoku precisely with threat-space search (TSS): the attacker plays only threatening moves, and the defender is allowed to cover all the threat cells at once. Such a search found the winning sequence in about **0.1 s of CPU** on early-90s hardware, and the solution tree for Gomoku took 138,790 nodes ([Allis et al., Go-Moku and Threat-Space Search](https://www.renju.se/rif/proof/Go-Moku.pdf)). Modern engines reduce this to VCF/VCT inside or at the leaves of alpha-beta: this is how Rapfi, Katagomo and even the minimal Squirrel from Gomocup 2023 (alpha-beta, iterative deepening, VCF/VCT, TT) are built ([Gomocup 2023](https://gomocup.org/results/gomocup-result-2023)). With gravity such a search is even cheaper: a threat is "live" only if the cell below it is occupied, so the defender usually has exactly one reply. Our conclusion: **forced search (own wins, mandatory blocks, double threats and, above all, winning or saving explosions) is the most valuable tactical extension**. It is the analogue of the chess extension on check and of quiescence search ([Chessprogramming: Quiescence Search](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Quiescence_Search)).

### MCTS loses to alpha-beta on a short limit in tactically sharp games

Ramanujan and co-authors showed that UCT misses "shallow traps", that is, a forced loss a few plies deep that full-width minimax sees. That is why MCTS wins in Go and minimax in chess ([Ramanujan et al., ICAPS 2010](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICAPS/article/view/13437)). Baier and Winands found such traps throughout a Connect-4 game. The best hybrid scheme (depth-2 minimax in playouts) gave **73.2% against baseline MCTS-Solver** with a third of the simulations ([Baier & Winands](https://dke.maastrichtuniversity.nl/m.winands/documents/paper%2049.pdf)). Pure UCT with 10,000 random playouts scored only **19.8% wins** in a Connect-4 tournament against nearly perfect alpha-beta agents ([Scheiermann & Konen, arXiv 2204.13307](https://arxiv.org/html/2204.13307v3)). The strength of pure MCTS on Connect 4 plateaus at about 1600 simulations ([AlphaViT, arXiv 2408.13871](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.13871)). In Breakthrough MCTS catches up with alpha-beta only as time grows: 27.6% at 1 s, 47.6% at 10 s ([Lanctot et al., arXiv 1406.0486](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1406.0486)).

The CodinGame counterargument is real: MCTS with a solver wins there at 100 ms. But UTTT is a wide game with a hard static evaluation, and in Connect 4 darkhorse64 has C++ and about 2 million games per second. For Quantum Connect everything tilts toward alpha-beta. There are more traps, because an explosion with its "any line" rule creates instant wins. The branching is small: 12 drops plus up to 12 explosions. A good window-based evaluation is easy to write. The time is 100 ms in Java with a cold JIT. In addition, in our assumption, random games of "six" on 8 rows will produce many draws and a weak signal. Our conclusion: **the foundation is alpha-beta; MCTS-Solver with decisive moves makes sense to keep only as an experiment**, if self-play shows that alpha-beta systematically errs in long-term strategy.

| Finding from other games | Source | Transfer to QC (6 in a row, 8×12, 3×3 explosion, 100 ms) |
|---|---|---|
| Bitboards, TT, center-first, ordering by threats | Pons, Schaeffer | **Directly**, but 2+ words per board, generic geometry, TT key with cooldowns |
| Iterative deepening, soft and hard limit | chess | **Directly**, deadline ~60–70 ms, no allocations |
| VCF/TSS threat search | Gomoku, Connect6 | **With adaptation**: "fives" accounting for gravity plus explosions as forcing moves |
| Pattern table over lines | Rapfi, Logistello | **With adaptation**: a table of states of 6-cell windows (3⁶ = 729 states) |
| Allis threat parity | Connect Four | **Partly**: only on a horizon without charged explosions, weight set by tuning |
| Solving the game, deep book | Tromp, Kaggle | **No**: the game is not solved and is non-monotonic |
| Null-move pruning | chess | **Risky**: passing is not allowed, and there is parity-based zugzwang |
| Endgame databases | Chinook | **No** |
| Pure MCTS | CodinGame, Go | **Rather no** at 100 ms; MCTS-Solver only as an experiment |

## The special move decides games, and the literature is silent about such an explosion

The closest studied variant with piece removal is PopOut: instead of a drop, a player pops out their own bottom piece. Pekkala's master's thesis (Oulu, 2014) solved 7×6 PopOut as a win for the first player using proof-number search. Plain alpha-beta needed restrictions there: the first player was forbidden non-winning pop-outs, and games that were too long were counted as his loss. The reasons are tree depth, a game without guaranteed termination, and the graph history interaction problem caused by repeated positions ([Oulu repository](https://oulurepo.oulu.fi/handle/10024/39813)). The text is available only as an abstract. The GHI problem was described by Kishimoto and Müller: a value from the TT can be wrong for the current path if it depends on history ([Kishimoto & Müller, AAAI 2004](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2004/AAAI04-102.pdf)). In Quantum Connect the situation is milder. The 10-move cooldown means that between explosions the board only grows, and repetition is not penalized by the rules. Only the 1000-move limit remains path-dependent, so GHI is a secondary risk as long as the game is far from the limit.

Other analogues give only intuition. In Hasbro Power Up, the "anvil" knocks out pieces beneath it, and the "bomb" removes an opponent's piece, but descriptions differ between sources and there is no AI work on them ([Wikipedia: Connect Four](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connect_Four), [Rarepike variants](https://rarepike.com/four/variants/)). A fan game on itch.io contains almost our rule: a bomb every 8 moves that destroys a column, also with no description of the AI ([itch.io Connect 4](https://xanderath.itch.io/connect-4)). In atomic chess a capture blows up both the capturing piece and its neighbors ([Chessprogramming: Atomic Chess](https://chessprogramming.org/Atomic_Chess)). This is a direct parallel to the fact that our explosion also destroys the piece just placed: an explosion is always an exchange, not a pure loss for the opponent. In Pommerman the bomb ammunition is part of the agent's observed state ([Pommerman README](https://github.com/MultiAgentLearning/playground/blob/master/pommerman/README.md)). This is the same principle as "the cooldown counter in the state and in the hash". No source gives "save or spend" heuristics for a limited ability. This is a real gap, not a shortcoming of the search.

The good news is that our problem is covered by brute force. Each explosion affects at most three columns. There are at most 12 explosion options per move, and each is simulated in microseconds. So on every move the bot can fully check: "do I have an explosion after which any 6-line appears on the board" and "after my candidate move, does the opponent have such an explosion, if his cooldown is ready". That is 12 × 12 = 144 simulations, a negligible share of the budget. From this follow three rules that we derive from the mechanics (there are no sources under them, and they need to be checked with self-play). The first: an explosion is always generated in the search as an ordinary move when the cooldown allows it, and is ordered right after immediate wins if it wins or removes a double threat. The second: when the opponent's explosion is charged or will be charged within the horizon, nearly complete lines of **both colors** are treated as his weapon. So the evaluation must penalize configurations that a shift of neighboring columns by 1–3 cells turns into a six. The third: the evaluation needs the terms "my explosion is ready" and "moves until the opponent's explosion is ready". The chess analogy is a bonus for a retained castling right, but the weight will have to be set by tuning.

There are two engineering obligations. First, the win check after an explosion must scan the whole board, not the lines through the drop point: the engine does the same. Second, the decision about the special move must be made in `accelerateQuark` and remembered, while `useSpecialMove` should only return the stored flag. The flag must not be raised if the column is invalid: otherwise the engine will crash ([engine bytecode](<notes/engine_rules_from_bytecode.md>)). The call order in the engine allows one more trick. `useSpecialMove` receives the board before the move is applied, but by that time our move has already been chosen, so its 100 ms can be spent on a preliminary search of the opponent's replies and on filling the TT. Before using this, it should be agreed with the organizers as a question of fair play.

## Our own neural network: AlphaZero does not pay off, a trainable pattern table does

The deep path is expensive even on a small board. Oracle's AlphaZero for Connect Four 6×7 required **77 GPU-hours before tuning and 21 after** ([Oracle Devs part 6](https://medium.com/oracledevs/lessons-from-alpha-zero-part-6-hyperparameter-tuning-b1cfcbe4ca9a), from a search excerpt). AlphaZero.jl: **75,000 self-play games**, 600 simulations per move, a ResNet of about 1.6 million parameters, 1–2 hours per iteration on an RTX 2070 ([AlphaZero.jl tutorial](https://jonathan-laurent.github.io/AlphaZero.jl/stable/tutorial/connect_four/)). Clausen and co-authors spent 2–6 hours on 20 cloud GPUs ([Clausen et al., ICAART 2021](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2021/102459/102459.pdf)). The result is still imperfect. Vanilla AlphaZero departs from the optimal line against an exact solver, and even learning from solver hints does not give perfect play ([arXiv 2607.08984](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08984)). The hybrid "AlphaZero plus minimax for tactics" won 58% against pure AlphaZero without a single loss in 100 games ([NTNU Two-Phase-Win](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/zh/publications/two-phase-win-strategy-for-improving-the-alphazeros-strength-2/), search excerpt). In ConnectX the best AlphaZero was rated below the agent with a solver and a book ([arXiv 2210.08263](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.08263v1)).

For Quantum Connect three hard constraints are added. The first is inference in Java. Our estimate (not a measurement): an AlphaZero.jl-level network on an 8×12 board is about 0.28 GFLOP per evaluation, that is, a handful of evaluations in 100 ms against the hundreds needed. The Vector API in JDK 25 is still an incubator and requires the flag `--add-modules jdk.incubator.vector` at launch ([JEP 508](https://openjdk.org/jeps/508)), and we do not control the flags of the tournament JVM. The second is the board size. The ConnectX AlphaZero implementation padded the board to 12×12 to survive different sizes ([arXiv 2210.08263](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.08263v1)). With us the size may turn out to be 8×12, 25×25 or something else, and a network trained on one breaks on another. The third is hardware: the laptop has an Iris Xe without CUDA. By extrapolation from the notes, training for 8×12 with explosions would need 3–10 times more compute than for 6×7, and a bridge between the Java self-play engine and a Python trainer.

The cheap path is confirmed by data and fits perfectly. Scheiermann and Konen trained an n-tuple network for Connect Four with TD without MCTS during training: 10 agents in **1.4 days on a single laptop CPU**, about 3.4 hours per agent. A move evaluation without search took about 0.05 ms. With an MCTS wrapper of 1000 iterations (~30 ms) the agent won **99/0/1 as first player against the nearly perfect alpha-beta AB-DL**, and 91/2/7 without the wrapper. In Othello the same method with the wrapper beat Edax up to level 7, and without search could not beat even level 2 ([Scheiermann & Konen, arXiv 2204.13307](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.13307)). There is an important caveat: on 6×7 the first player theoretically wins, so these wins measure absence of mistakes rather than superiority over strong alpha-beta. An n-tuple network is simply a set of tables over groups of cells. For "six" the natural set of groups is the 134 winning windows themselves, that is, the same pattern table on which Logistello and the distilled Rapfi are built. Texel tuning (logistic regression of the game result on the static evaluation) gave a chess engine about **+99.6 Elo in total** on ~400 parameters and 64 thousand fast games ([Chessprogramming: Texel's Tuning Method](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Texel%27s_Tuning_Method)). NNUE is a proven way to put a network inside alpha-beta: the first-layer accumulator is updated by adding rows of weights, and on a mass change (for us, an explosion) it is recomputed entirely ([nnue-pytorch docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/official-stockfish/nnue-pytorch/refs/heads/master/docs/nnue.md)). But NNUE must be trained on the evaluations of an already strong search, so it is the second stage, not the first.

The answer to the question "should we train our own neural network" therefore splits in two. A deep AlphaZero-style network, no: the costs are one to two orders of magnitude higher, inference in Java does not fit in 100 ms, portability to other board sizes is zero, and the data say that even a finished network loses to hybrids with alpha-beta. A trainable evaluation, yes, but later and in the simplest form: the weights of the window pattern table and of the "cooldown/parity" terms, tuned by TD or Texel tuning on self-play of our own Java engine. Python and a GPU are not needed for this. If profiling later shows that the weak point is the evaluation and not the tactics, the next step is a small NNUE distilled from deep search. Every change should be verified by self-play with SPRT, as is customary in chess engines ([Chessprogramming: SPRT](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Sequential_Probability_Ratio_Test)). Since the engine does not swap sides by itself, the harness must run each pair of bots in both orders and with random openings.

## Conclusion

The main shift relative to the original framing: Quantum Connect turns out to be not "a bigger Connect Four" but a Gomoku-like gravity game in which ordinary drops probably make it hard to beat a careful opponent, and **the decisive mechanism is the periodic explosion with the rule "any line counts for the player who triggered it"**. Therefore the most valuable part of the world's experience is not Connect Four solvers or AlphaZero, but the combination of "fast alpha-beta + full search of forcing moves" shared by Yixin, Rapfi, NCTU6 and Pons's solver. Plus one detail new to the literature: modeling the opponent's explosion as a weapon against nearly complete lines of both colors. The strength of the future bot should be measured first of all by two metrics that the literature does not provide: the share of draws between two copies of the bot (this tests the hypothesis that "six" is a draw) and the share of games decided by a special move.

In practice this means the following order of work. First reliability: not a single timeout, not a single invalid special move, generic board geometry. Then 1-move checks of wins and explosions. Then alpha-beta with a TT and forced extension. Then a self-play harness with SPRT. And only after that TD/Texel tuning of the pattern table. Against a field that, judging by the format of the CERN event, wrote bots in 45 minutes, the first two steps will give more than any neural network. The real tournament parameters remain unknown (board size, limits, scoring, side swapping, whether using the `useSpecialMove` time is allowed), and they need to be obtained from the organizers before optimizing anything for 8×12.
